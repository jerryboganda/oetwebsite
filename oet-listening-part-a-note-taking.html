OET Listening Part A: note-taking and spelling

Part A asks you to turn a live consultation into accurate notes, hearing it once. This guide covers prediction, what to write and what not to write, spelling and abbreviations, and a worked practice extract with the distractors marked.

By Dr Ahmed Hesham, medical doctor and OET educator · Published 27 October 2026

Transcript to answer

Predict the gap, wait for the final word, write it exactly.

Part A rewards accurate recording of detail. The right answer is usually one to three words the speaker actually says, sometimes after an earlier detail that the speaker then changes.

What OET Listening Part A asks you to do

The real question candidates ask is: I understood the conversation, so why did I lose marks? In Part A, understanding is only half the task. The other half is writing the right words, in the right form, with acceptable spelling.

The format, from OET

OET Listening lasts approximately 40 minutes, with three parts and 42 questions. Part A has two recordings, each a health professional talking to a patient, lasting about four to five minutes. Each has 12 questions, so Part A carries 24 of the 42 marks. You complete a health professional’s notes with a word or short phrase you hear.

The timing, from OET

You have 30 seconds to look at the notes before each recording starts, and you hear each recording once only. On paper you get two minutes at the end of the Listening test to check answers for all three parts; on computer and OET@Home there is a short checking pause built in.

What to write

OET says to complete the notes using the same words you hear, without paraphrasing or changing the information. Answers are generally one to three words. Its own example: if the patient says she was sent to an osteopath, both “osteopath” and “an osteopath” are acceptable, but you must not change the word form or add extra words.

What loses marks

According to OET, you lose marks if you repeat words already in the notes, add information that is not in the recording, contradict yourself or make your meaning unclear. Grammar mistakes can also cost marks, and on paper Part A is marked by human assessors, so your writing must be legible.

Spelling and abbreviations: what OET says, and what it does not

Spelling rules in Listening are more flexible than in Reading, but they are not relaxed. Here is what the official guidance says, and where it is silent.

UK and US spellings

OET accepts both UK and US spellings in Listening Part A, and says minor spelling variations may be accepted as long as the intended meaning is clear.

But spelling still counts

OET also says you may lose marks for spelling mistakes, and that you should be able to spell medical words in common public use, such as diabetes or cholesterol. Treat accurate spelling as the target, not the flexibility.

When you cannot spell a word

OET advises writing it phonetically, as it sounded, so you do not miss what comes next, then correcting it during the checking time.

Abbreviations: the official position

OET’s published Listening guidance asks you to use the same words you hear, and we could not find an official list of accepted abbreviations for Listening. The Reading rule, that abbreviations are not accepted unless they appear in the text, applies to Reading only.

Our cautious advice

If the speaker says the short form, such as ECG, write what you heard. If you abbreviate a longer phrase to keep up, expand it to the full words in the checking time. Do not rely on your own shorthand as a final answer.

Capital letters

OET confirms you may write Listening Part A answers in block capitals, as long as you are consistent and do not switch between capitals and lower case.

A step-by-step method from transcript to answer

OET notes that everything on the question sheet is mentioned in order, usually in different words, and that the questions come in order too. This method uses that structure.

  • 1. Use the 30 seconds to predict. Read the headings and the words around each gap. Decide the type of answer: a time period, a body part, a medicine, a number, a job, a symptom.
  • 2. Follow the headings as signposts. When the speaker moves to the next heading, move your eyes with them. If you hear the next heading’s topic, the earlier gap has passed.
  • 3. Wait for the final version. Patients often correct themselves or mention an earlier option first. Write the detail the speaker settles on.
  • 4. Write the speaker’s words, briefly. One to three words, in the form you heard. Do not repeat words already printed in the notes.
  • 5. Check sense and grammar. OET advises checking that each answer makes logical and grammatical sense in the gap.
  • 6. If you miss one, let it go. Keep up with the audio. Missing one answer is better than losing the next three while you think about it.

Practice example: a consultation extract and note gaps

Practice example only. This short extract, the patient and the notes were invented for teaching. A real Part A recording is longer, with 12 gaps and a range of accents. Read the notes first, predict each gap, then read the extract.

Practice notes: practice nurse, patient Mr Daniel Price

(1) Night-time breathlessness for approximately ______. (2) Recently started work in a ______. (3) Reliever inhaler now used ______ a day. (4) Stopped preventer inhaler due to ______. (5) Mother has ______. (6) Home monitoring with a ______.

Practice extract

Nurse: What’s brought you in today? Patient: My breathing’s been bad at night. I’d say it started about a week ago. Actually, no, it’s closer to three weeks now. Nurse: Has anything changed at work? Patient: I started a new job. I was in a warehouse before, but now I’m working in a bakery, so there’s flour everywhere. Nurse: How often are you using the blue reliever? Patient: Twice a day at first, but this week it’s been four times a day. Nurse: And the brown preventer? Patient: I stopped it. I thought it was making me put on weight, but really it was the sore throat. It kept giving me a sore throat. Nurse: Any family history? Patient: My dad had hay fever, and my mum’s got eczema. Nurse: Do you monitor at home? Patient: Yes, I’ve got a peak flow meter. I use it most mornings.

From prediction to answer

  • (1) three weeks. Predicted: a time period. Distractor: “a week”, which the patient immediately corrects. Writing both would contradict yourself.
  • (2) bakery. Predicted: a workplace, a noun after “a”. Distractor: warehouse, his previous job. “Flour” is wrong because it does not fit “work in a” grammatically.
  • (3) four times. Predicted: a frequency. Distractor: twice, which was at first. Do not add “a day”, because it is already printed in the notes.
  • (4) sore throat. Predicted: a side effect. Distractor: putting on weight, which the patient rejects. Writing “weight gain” would also be a paraphrase rather than his words.
  • (5) eczema. Predicted: a condition. Distractor: hay fever, which belongs to his father. If you are unsure of the spelling, write it as it sounds and correct it in the checking time; this is a common word you are expected to spell.
  • (6) peak flow meter. Predicted: a device. Write the full phrase. A personal abbreviation such as PFM is not what the speaker said, so expand any shortcut before time runs out.

Common Part A mistakes that cost marks

Use these as labels when you mark a practice test. Knowing which type of error you make most often tells you what to drill next.

Writing the first answer you hear

Speakers often give an earlier time, dose or option before the final one. Wait until the speaker settles on the detail.

Paraphrasing

Swapping in a medical term for the patient’s words, or summarising, changes the answer. Write what was said.

Repeating the printed words

If the notes already say “a day” or “pain in”, your answer should not repeat them.

Leaving shorthand uncorrected

Shortcuts help you keep pace, but the final answer should be clear to an assessor. Expand them in the checking time.

Misspelling everyday medical words

Common terms such as diabetes, eczema or cholesterol should be spelled correctly. Keep a personal list from each practice test.

Stopping after a missed gap

Losing one answer is normal. Losing the next three because you are still thinking about it is avoidable.

Key takeaway

Use the 30 seconds to predict the type of word for each gap, follow the headings, and wait for the speaker’s final version before writing. Write the speaker’s own words in one to three words, then use the checking time to fix spelling and expand any shortcuts.

OET Listening Part A FAQ

Short answers to the questions candidates ask most about this part of the test.

How many questions are in OET Listening Part A?

Part A has two consultation recordings with 12 questions each, so 24 questions in total. You complete a health professional's notes with a word or short phrase you hear.

Are spelling mistakes penalised in Listening Part A?

They can be. OET accepts UK and US spellings and says minor variations may be accepted if the meaning is clear, but you may still lose marks for spelling mistakes.

Can I use abbreviations in Listening Part A?

OET asks you to write the words you hear and does not publish a list of accepted abbreviations for Listening. Write short forms only if the speaker uses them, and expand your own shorthand before time runs out.

Do I hear the Part A recordings twice?

No. Each recording is played once. You have 30 seconds to read the notes before each extract, so use that time to predict the type of answer for each gap.
