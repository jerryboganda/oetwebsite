OET Study Plan: 4, 8 and 12-Week Schedules

How long you need depends on how far you are from your target grade and how many hours you can give each week. Pick the plan that matches your starting point, follow it sub-test by sub-test, and let your mock results tell you when to adjust.

Which plan fits your starting point?

OET’s own advice is that candidates whose recent results are already close to grade B may need only a few weeks to get used to the test format, while candidates at C+ or C need more time to improve. If you can study only a few hours a week, plan for longer. Choose from evidence: a recent OET result, a full mock or the free placement test.

4 weeks: near your target

A recent result or full mock puts you at your target in most sub-tests, one is just short, and you know the format. The work is technique, timing and one weakness.

8 weeks: one or two grades short

One or two sub-tests sit a grade or two below your target, for example C+ or C where you need B. There is time for real skill work and a full mock every two to three weeks.

12 weeks: building from below B

Several sub-tests are below B, or your placement result shows language gaps in more than one skill. The first weeks build grammar, vocabulary and stamina. If most skills are B1 or below, the placement test page explains a foundation-first route.

Before week 1: set your baseline

Spend the first two or three days on these five steps, whichever plan you choose.

1. Confirm your target

Check the exact grade your regulator needs in each sub-test, and whether it must come from one sitting, in the OET score requirements guide.

2. Take the placement test

If you have no recent OET result, take the free placement test before week 1. It takes about 60 to 85 minutes and gives an indicative CEFR level for Listening, Reading, Writing and Speaking.

3. Learn the format on a sample

OET advises new test-takers to work through an official sample test slowly and carefully. Our free OET sample tests guide and the free practice resources show where to start.

4. Read the method once

Read the Writing, Speaking, Reading and Listening guides before you time yourself, so practice tests a method, not a guess.

5. Fix the end date

OET suggests counting the weeks to your test on a calendar and blocking out work and family commitments before adding study. Check OET test dates and fees so your plan ends on a real date.

The 4-week plan: near your target

Four weeks is for sharpening, not rebuilding. Put most of your time into the sub-test that is short.

How many hours a week? Suggestions, not rules

OET suggests finding at least one hour a day to study, as long as the schedule is realistic and still leaves time for commuting, rest, meals and sleep. The figures below are our suggestions; adjust them around your shifts.

Suggested weekly time
  • 4-week plan: around 12–15 hours a week, roughly two hours on most days.
  • 8-week plan: around 8–12 hours a week, with longer sessions at the weekend.
  • 12-week plan: around 7–10 hours a week, with at least half of weeks 1–3 on general language.
How to split the time

Give about half your time to the sub-test furthest from your target and share the rest so the others do not slip. Writing and Speaking need feedback from someone who knows the criteria.

Why the mocks are spaced out

OET recommends one practice test every two to three weeks, fixing the skills behind your errors in between; test after test builds test-taking skills, not language. A full mock is about two and three-quarter hours of test time, so plan it like a shift.

Common study-plan mistakes

Check your plan against this list at the end of every week.

Mock after mock, no review

A mock only diagnoses. Improvement comes after it, when you sort every lost mark by type and practise that skill.

Equal time for unequal gaps

Giving all four sub-tests the same hours when one is two grades short.

Writing letters nobody marks

Ten unmarked letters can repeat one selection error ten times.

Practising Speaking silently

Reading model role-plays is not practice. Say each one aloud, within 3 minutes of preparation and about 5 of speaking, and record it.

Choosing the plan by the calendar

Forcing a 12-week gap into four weeks. If your mocks are not moving, move the date, not the standard.

Cramming in the final week

New material in the last days adds stress, not marks.

Key takeaway

Choose your plan from evidence, weight your time towards the weakest sub-test, get feedback on Writing and Speaking, and space full mocks two to three weeks apart so each one measures real progress.

OET study plan FAQ

Short answers to the planning questions candidates ask most.

How long does it take to prepare for OET?

It depends on your starting level and your weekly study time. OET says candidates already close to grade B may need only a few weeks to get used to the format, while candidates at C+ or C need more time. If you can study only a few hours a week, plan for longer.

How many hours a day should I study for OET?

OET suggests at least one hour a day if that is realistic for you. Our plans suggest roughly 7 to 15 hours a week, but rest and sleep matter too, and a schedule you can keep beats one you abandon.

How many mock tests should I take before OET?

OET recommends one practice test every two to three weeks, spending the time in between on the skills that caused your errors. Repeating tests on their own improves test-taking skills, not language skills, so review each mock before you sit the next.

Can I prepare for OET in 4 weeks?

Yes, if a recent result or full mock shows you are already near your target in most sub-tests and you know the format. Four weeks is mainly for exam technique and one or two weak areas, not for building general English.

When should I take the free placement test?

Take it before week 1 if you have no recent OET result. It gives an indicative CEFR level for each skill, which helps you choose a plan and shows which sub-test to prioritise.
