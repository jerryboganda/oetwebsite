OET Speaking: How to Handle an Angry or Anxious Patient

When the patient or relative on the card is angry, upset or frightened, many candidates either argue or apologise without moving forward. This guide maps a calm, structured response to OET’s clinical communication criteria, with an invented role-play card, a model dialogue and phrases you can adapt.

Why OET includes angry and anxious patients

A question candidates often ask is: "What if the interlocutor shouts at me?" OET’s own Speaking page warns that you may meet anxious or angry patients in the role-plays, because these situations are part of real healthcare work.

How the sub-test works

Speaking takes about 20 minutes and has two profession-specific role-plays of about 5 minutes each. You get three minutes to read each card, can make notes on it and can ask the interlocutor about unfamiliar words. The interlocutor does not assess you: the session is recorded and the audio is assessed by two OET assessors.

Read the emotion during preparation

OET advises looking for adjectives in the background information on your card and putting yourself in the patient’s shoes. If the card says “angry”, “frustrated” or “worried”, plan your first response before the role-play starts.

What anger usually hides

Anger in a role-play often sits on top of worry: about pain, about being ignored, or about losing control of what happens next. Your task is not to win the argument but to find that worry and address it.

You do not have to finish every task

OET says there is no penalty for not completing all the elements on the role card. If the patient needs more time to calm down, a well-handled conversation is better evidence than rushing through the list.

How the clinical communication criteria reward a calm response

OET assesses Speaking against four linguistic criteria, each marked out of 6, and five clinical communication criteria, each marked out of 3. Handling an upset patient well produces evidence for all five.

Tone is assessed too

The four linguistic criteria are Intelligibility, Fluency, Appropriateness of Language, and Resources of Grammar and Expression. Appropriateness of Language covers register, tone and lexis for the context, so a defensive or cold tone with an upset relative costs marks even when the grammar is accurate.

A five-step method for an angry or upset patient

Use the same order every time. Most problems in difficult role-plays come from doing step 5 before steps 1 and 2.

1. Let the first wave finish

Do not interrupt the opening complaint with facts. Stay quiet, then show you heard it. Interrupting usually raises the temperature.

2. Acknowledge the feeling and its reason

Use their own words: “You took the day off and nobody phoned you.” A short apology for their experience is fine; an apology followed by an excuse is not.

3. Do not argue, defend or blame

Avoid “That’s not my fault” or blaming another team. You can be honest about what happened without making it anyone’s fault.

4. Find the worry underneath

Ask one open question, such as “What worries you most about the delay?”, then respond to the answer, not to the tone.

5. Explain, involve and agree

Signpost, explain in small chunks, check understanding, offer real options and agree the next step. Never promise what you cannot control.

Angry or anxious: what changes

With an anxious patient the order is the same, but the emphasis shifts. Slow down, use shorter sentences, ask what they already know before explaining, and check understanding more often. With an angry patient, acknowledgement must come before any explanation. In both cases, “don’t worry” without information dismisses the feeling.

Practice role-play card: a cancelled operation

Invented practice card written by OET with Dr Hesham for teaching. It is not official OET material and does not describe real people.

Candidate card (nurse)

Setting: Orthopaedic ward, hospital. You are talking to the son of Mr Arthur Pryce, 84, admitted two days ago with a fractured hip. His operation was cancelled at 11 am because an emergency case needed the theatre. He has not eaten since midnight. His son has just arrived and is very angry.

  • Acknowledge the son’s frustration and find out his main concerns.
  • Explain why the operation was postponed, without blaming colleagues.
  • Explain the new plan: first on tomorrow morning’s list; he can eat and drink now.
  • Respond to concerns about his father’s confusion and pain.
  • Agree how the son will be kept informed.
Interlocutor card (son)

You took the day off work. Nobody phoned you about the cancellation. Your father has had no food all day and seemed muddled when you arrived.

  • Start angrily: demand to know why nobody told you.
  • Become calmer if the nurse acknowledges your frustration and explains clearly.
  • Say your father is “not himself” and seems to be in pain.
  • Ask whether the operation will definitely go ahead tomorrow.
Practise with your own profession

The same method works on every profession’s cards. Try it on our nursing, medicine, pharmacy and physiotherapy role-play cards.

Model dialogue excerpt

The first two minutes of the invented card above. Notice how little information the nurse gives until the son feels heard.

Model dialogue (invented practice card)

Nurse: Good afternoon, I’m the nurse looking after your father today. Are you Mr Pryce’s son?

Son: Yes, and I want to know why nobody rang me. I took the day off, Dad’s had nothing to eat since midnight, and now the operation’s off?

Nurse: You took the day off, your father has gone without food all day, and nobody phoned you about the change. I can see why you’re angry, and I’m sorry you found out this way.

Son: Sorry doesn’t really help, does it?

Nurse: It doesn’t change what happened, I know. Could I explain why it was postponed, and then we can talk about what happens next for your dad?

Son: Go on, then.

Nurse: An emergency patient needed the theatre urgently this morning. That’s not the news anyone wanted. Your father is now first on tomorrow morning’s list, and he can eat and drink straight away.

Son: But he was muddled when I came in. He’s never like that.

Nurse: That must have been worrying to see. What did you notice?

Son: He didn’t know what day it was. And he winces every time he moves.

Nurse: Thank you, that’s really helpful. Confusion can happen in older people after a fall, especially with pain or without food. I’ll ask the doctor to see him this afternoon and review his pain relief. So your main worries are his confusion, his pain and not being kept informed. Is that right?

Son: Yes. And will it definitely happen tomorrow?

Nurse: I can’t promise that, because emergencies can’t be predicted, but he is first on the list. Would it help to speak to the surgeon this afternoon? And which number should we call, so you hear any news straight away?

What the nurse is doing
  • Opening: greets and confirms who they are speaking to before anything else (Relationship-building).
  • First reply: reflects the son’s three complaints in his own terms, then names the feeling and apologises once.
  • “Sorry doesn’t help”: the nurse agrees rather than defends, then signposts the conversation (Providing structure).
  • The explanation: one reason, no blame, then the plan in two short sentences.
  • “He’s never like that”: the nurse picks up the cue and asks an open question instead of reassuring (patient’s perspective).
  • Summary: the nurse checks the three concerns are right (Information-gathering).
  • The hard question: an honest answer, then an offer and a request for a contact number that involve him (Information-giving).
Key takeaway

The son calms down because he is heard, not because he is told to. Acknowledge first, explain second, and let his worries decide the order of the rest of the conversation.

Common mistakes with angry or anxious patients

Each of these makes a difficult role-play harder, even when the language is accurate.

“Please calm down”

It tells the person their feeling is the problem. Acknowledge the reason for the feeling instead.

Defending or blaming

“It wasn’t my decision, theatre cancelled it” sounds dismissive and unprofessional. Explain the reason without assigning fault.

Correcting before acknowledging

Opening with facts (“Actually, we did try to call”) turns the conversation into an argument.

Over-promising

“I promise it will happen tomorrow” buys a moment of calm and loses trust later. Say what you can do.

Apologising in circles

Repeated apologies with no plan leave you without structure. Apologise once, then signpost what happens next.

Missing the second worry

While dealing with the anger, candidates often miss the quieter cue, such as “he’s not himself”. That cue is often the real task.

Angry or anxious patient FAQ

Short answers to the questions candidates ask about difficult OET role-plays.

Will the interlocutor really act angry in an OET Speaking role-play?

Sometimes. OET says you may experience anxious or angry patients in the role-plays, as in real healthcare settings. Stay calm, acknowledge the emotion and keep the conversation structured, as you would at work.

Which OET criteria does handling an angry patient affect?

Mostly the five clinical communication criteria: Relationship-building, Understanding and incorporating the patient's perspective, Providing structure, Information-gathering and Information-giving, each marked out of 3. Tone and register also count under the linguistic criterion Appropriateness of Language.

Should I apologise to an angry patient in OET Speaking?

A short, sincere apology for the person's experience is appropriate, for example that nobody told them about a delay. Avoid blaming colleagues or apologising repeatedly. Then move on to explaining what happened and what happens next.

What if the patient is still upset and I run out of time?

OET says there is no penalty for not completing all the elements on the role card. Keep acknowledging, explaining and agreeing a next step. A calm, structured conversation gives better evidence of your skills than rushing through every task.

How is an anxious patient different from an angry one?

An anxious patient usually needs a slower pace, shorter explanations and more checking of understanding. An angry patient usually needs their frustration acknowledged before they can listen. In both cases, find the worry underneath and relate your explanation to it.
