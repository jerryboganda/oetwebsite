Listening Parts B and C

OET Listening Parts B and C: prediction, opinion and distractors

In Parts B and C, speakers paraphrase, change their minds and mention every option. This guide shows you how to predict from the question and options, separate a speaker’s opinion from the facts they report, and choose the option the recording actually supports.

The format of Listening Parts B and C

The Listening sub-test lasts about 40 minutes and has 42 questions in three parts, and each recording is heard once. Part A carries 24 marks, Part B 6 and Part C 12. In Parts B and C you choose one of three options.

Part B: six short workplace extracts

Each recording lasts about 45 seconds and has one or two speakers, one of whom is always a healthcare professional. OET gives examples such as part of a handover, a training session, a staff briefing, a discussion between colleagues, or a conversation between a patient and a health professional. You have 15 seconds to read the question before each extract and about five seconds afterwards to choose.

Part C: two longer recordings

Each is a presentation or an interview of around four to five minutes, involving at least one healthcare professional and similar to professional development content. Each recording has six questions that follow the order of the audio, and you have 90 seconds to read them before it starts.

Answering on paper or computer

On paper you shade the circle next to your answer; answers written elsewhere are not marked. On computer and OET@Home you click an option and can right-click to eliminate one.

Checking time

On paper there are two minutes at the end to check all three parts; on computer there is a short pause after each question. A wrong answer costs no marks, so never leave a blank.

What the questions ask

OET lists what each part tests. The type of stem tells you what evidence to listen for. The example stems are our own.

Part B: five things it tests

  • Gist: “The doctor is mainly telling the nurse about…”
  • Detail: “When will the dressing next be changed?”
  • Purpose: “Why is the physiotherapist calling?”
  • Opinion or attitude: “What does the manager think of the new rota?”
  • Next action: “What will the pharmacist do next?”

Part C: meaning across a longer talk

  • Gist: the main point of a section of the talk
  • Opinion: what the speaker thinks about something
  • Attitude: how the speaker feels about it: concerned, surprised, cautiously positive
  • Two stem shapes: a direct question, or an incomplete sentence you can turn into a question

Prediction from the stem and the options

Fifteen seconds is enough for one question, and 90 seconds enough to map a Part C talk, if you use the same four steps.

1. Read the context

In Part B, the context statement tells you who is speaking and where, so you can expect a handover, a briefing or a patient conversation. In Part C, read all six questions first: together they show the structure of the talk.

2. Find the focus in the stem

Underline the word that limits the answer: a time (“today”, not yesterday), a person (“the doctor’s view”, not the patient’s) or a type (a reason, a concern, a next step).

3. Spot what separates the options

Options often share a topic and differ in one element: who acts, when, or how strongly. Name that difference in one or two words. It is what you are listening for.

4. Expect paraphrase

OET says the audio uses synonyms and parallel expressions, not the wording of the question. If an option says “reduce”, be ready for “cut back” or “bring down”. In Part C, the interviewer’s next question tells you to move on.

Tracking opinion and fact

Speakers often report a fact or someone else’s view first, then give their own. Learn to hear the switch.

Reported facts and other people’s views

“The guidelines say…”, “Studies have shown…”, “A lot of colleagues assume…”, “People sometimes describe it as…”. True in the recording, but not necessarily the speaker’s opinion.

The speaker’s own view

“For me…”, “What worries me is…”, “I’m not convinced…”, “If I’m honest…”, “The real issue is…”. These usually carry the answer to an opinion question.

Contrast words

OET points to linking words such as “but” and “however”, which can change which option is correct. “Actually”, “that said” and “what we found was” often introduce the speaker’s real position.

Strength and hedging

“Useful in some cases” is not “essential”. “I can see why people say that” is acknowledgement, not agreement. Match the strength of the option to the strength of the speaker.

Six distractor patterns

OET notes that you will hear elements of every option and must choose the one that answers the question. Name the pattern behind each wrong choice.

1. Mentioned, but not the answer

A word from the option is in the audio, used in a different way. Hearing it proves nothing.

2. The first idea, later changed

“We were going to…, but…”. OET advises you to keep listening after you think you have the answer.

3. Someone else’s opinion

The speaker reports a view, then disagrees with it. The option matches the reported view.

4. Right detail, wrong time or person

Tomorrow’s plan offered for a question about today, or a colleague’s task as the speaker’s.

5. Half right

One part of the option matches the audio and another does not. OET says every element of an option must match.

6. Sensible, but not said

Good clinical practice that nobody in the recording states. Answer from the audio, not from experience.

Practice example: two invented transcript excerpts

Practice example only. Both excerpts, the speakers and the questions were invented by OET with Dr Hesham for teaching; they are not from an OET recording. A real Part C recording is much longer. Predict, then read.

Part B style (invented): a ward pharmacist talks to a nurse about a patient’s medication

Pharmacist: Morning. It’s about Mrs Adams in bed twelve. Her potassium came back a bit high this morning, so the team have held her ramipril for now. I’ve put a note on the chart.
Nurse: Right. Should I still give her the spironolactone? It’s due at ten.
Pharmacist: No, that’s on hold too. The doctor wanted to stop it altogether, actually, but we’ve agreed to wait for tomorrow’s bloods before deciding. What I do need from you is a call if anyone writes her up for a potassium supplement, because the night team might have done.

Question 1. What does the pharmacist ask the nurse to do?

A) stop the spironolactone permanently
B) arrange a repeat blood test for tomorrow
C) tell her if a potassium supplement has been prescribed

Question 1 worked: answer C

Focus: an action, and it is the nurse’s. A fails, first idea later changed: the doctor wanted to stop it, “but” they agreed to wait. B fails, right detail, wrong person: tomorrow’s bloods are the team’s plan; nobody asks the nurse to arrange them. C survives: “a call if anyone writes her up for a potassium supplement” is paraphrased as “tell her if a supplement has been prescribed”.

Part C style (invented): an interview with a community wound-care nurse about video reviews

Interviewer: Your service moved some wound reviews to video calls last year. How has that gone?
Nurse: Better than I expected, if I’m honest. When we started, I assumed the technology would be the main barrier for patients, and a few colleagues still think that. But what we actually found was that most people managed the calls fine once a family member had set things up. The bigger issue was image quality, so for any wound that’s getting worse we still go and see it in person.
Interviewer: So video isn’t replacing home visits?
Nurse: Not at all. People sometimes describe it as a cost-saving exercise, and I can see why, because fewer visits means less travel. But for me the real gain is speed. If a patient is worried on a Monday, we see the wound on Monday, not at the next visit on Thursday.

Question 2. What does the nurse say about patients using the technology?

A) It remains the main obstacle to video reviews.
B) Most coped well once someone helped them start.
C) Her colleagues now agree that it works well.

Question 2 worked: answer B

A fails, first idea and someone else’s view: “I assumed” was her starting belief, and only “a few colleagues still think that”. B survives: after “But what we actually found”: most managed “once a family member had set things up”. C fails, contradiction: some colleagues still see technology as the barrier.

Question 3. In the nurse’s opinion, the main benefit of video reviews is that they

A) reduce the time staff spend travelling.
B) allow patients’ concerns to be assessed sooner.
C) mean fewer home visits are needed.

Question 3 worked: answer B

A fails, acknowledgement is not agreement: “I can see why” reports the cost-saving view before “But for me”. B survives: “the real gain is speed”, shown by seeing the wound the same day. C fails, contradiction: she says “Not at all” to video replacing home visits.

Key takeaway

Every wrong option was mentioned in the audio. What decided each answer was a contrast word, “but”, “actually” or “for me”, followed by the speaker’s final position. Predict the difference between the options, then keep listening until the speaker settles.

A practice routine for Parts B and C

Built on OET’s preparation advice: natural speed, workplace language, stamina and one-sentence summaries.

Daily: three Part B extracts

Allow yourself exactly 15 seconds to read each question. Answer, then read the transcript and label each wrong option with its distractor pattern.

Every other day: one Part C recording

Use the full 90 seconds to map the six questions. Afterwards, write one sentence per section and check it against the transcript.

Weekly: stamina and language

Listen to healthcare talks and podcasts, increasing the length each week. Note phrases for agreement, concern, doubt, cause and effect.

Every two to three weeks: a full paper

Sit Parts A, B and C in one go, then sort lost marks by part and pattern. Train Part A note-taking alongside B and C.

OET Listening Parts B and C FAQ

Short answers to the questions candidates ask most about these two parts.

How many questions are in OET Listening Parts B and C?

Part B has six short workplace extracts with one three-option question each. Part C has two longer recordings, each with six three-option questions. Together they are worth 18 of the 42 Listening marks.

How long do I have to read the questions in Parts B and C?

OET says you have 15 seconds to read the question before each Part B extract and 90 seconds to read the questions before each Part C recording.

Are the OET Listening recordings played twice?

No. Each part is heard once, so use the reading time to predict and keep listening until the speaker settles on a final position.

What is the difference between Listening Part B and Part C?

Part B uses short workplace interactions of about 45 seconds, such as a handover or a staff briefing. Part C uses presentations or interviews of around four to five minutes and focuses more on the speakers’ opinions and attitudes.

Should I guess if I miss an answer in Part B or Part C?

Yes. OET says you will not lose marks for an incorrect answer, so choose the most likely option and move straight on to the next question.
