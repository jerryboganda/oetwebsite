OET Speaking: Realistic Empathy and Signposting

Empathy in an OET role-play should sound like a clinician noticing a real worry, not a line recited from a list. This guide maps empathy and signposting to OET's clinical communication criteria and shows a weak and a stronger version of the same short exchange.

By Dr Ahmed Hesham, medical doctor and OET educator · Published 27 October 2026

The habit in three moves

Hear the cue, name the feeling, then act on it.

Assessors hear only the audio, so your empathy and your structure both have to be audible in your words.

The real question: how do I show empathy without sounding fake?

Most candidates already know they should "show empathy". The problem is that it comes out as the same sentence every time, often at the wrong moment, and the patient's next worry is ignored.

How the sub-test works

OET describes Speaking as about 20 minutes with two profession-specific role-plays of around five minutes each. Before each role-play you get three minutes to read the card, make notes and ask the interlocutor about unfamiliar words. You can refer to the card throughout.

Why your words carry everything

The interlocutor does not assess you. The session is recorded and the audio is assessed by two OET assessors. OET's own Speaking guide points out that, because assessors only hear you, your empathy must be verbal. A sympathetic face earns nothing on a recording.

What candidates usually do instead

They say "I understand your concern" after every patient line. OET's Clinical Communication guide comments on a sample nurse who overuses exactly that phrase. Repetition tells the assessor you are reciting, not listening.

What OET's clinical communication criteria actually ask for

Speaking is assessed against Linguistic criteria and Clinical Communication criteria. The clinical communication side has five criteria, each scored from 0 (ineffective use) to 3 (adept use). Empathy and signposting appear in two of them, but they support all five.

Relationship-building

Includes initiating the interaction appropriately, an attentive and respectful attitude, a non-judgmental approach and showing empathy for feelings, predicament or emotional state.

Understanding and incorporating the patient's perspective

Eliciting ideas, concerns and expectations, picking up the patient's cues, and relating explanations to what the patient told you.

Providing structure

Sequencing the interview purposefully, signposting changes in topic and using organising techniques in explanations.

Information-gathering

Active listening, open then closed questions, no compound or leading questions, clarifying vague answers and summarising.

Information-giving

Finding out what the patient already knows, pausing while giving information, inviting reactions, checking understanding and asking what else they need.

What grade B looks like

OET states that candidates securing grade B have predominantly scored 5 out of 6 on each linguistic criterion and 2 out of 3 on the clinical communication criteria.

A step-by-step method you can use in every role-play

Empathy and signposting are easier when you plan where they will be needed. Use the three minutes of preparation for this, not for writing full sentences.

1. Mark the card during preparation

Number the tasks on the card so you can see the topic changes. Then circle the likely emotional moment, for example a fear of needles, a worry about work or frustration with waiting. Those two marks tell you where to signpost and where empathy will be needed.

2. Listen for the cue, not the script

A cue can be a whole sentence ("I live on my own") or a short sound ("Oh..."). OET's guide highlights a sample nurse who missed a patient's worried "Oh" while explaining insulin. When you hear one, stop and respond before you carry on explaining.

3. Name the specific feeling, then link it to help

Genuine empathy has three parts: reflect what they said, acknowledge why it matters, and offer something. "Being on your own after an operation is a real worry. Let's look at what support we can set up." Short, specific, followed by action.

4. Invite the patient to say more

A brief open question after empathy ("What worries you most about that?") turns the feeling into information. It also covers eliciting concerns, so one moment serves two criteria.

5. Signpost every change of topic

Before moving to the next task, tell the patient: "Now I'd like to talk about your medication." OET's guide notes that simple signposts such as "OK", "So" and "Now" work; what does not work is starting each new topic with "um".

6. Organise explanations and pause

Announce the shape: "There are two things to keep an eye on." Give one, pause, check, give the next. Pauses let the patient react, which gives you the next cue to respond to.

Practice example: weak and stronger versions of the same exchange

Practice example (invented for teaching, not a real OET card or a real patient). Setting: hospital ward. You are the nurse. Mrs Patel, 72, had a knee replacement four days ago and is going home tomorrow. Tasks: explain wound care and the exercise plan, and respond to her concerns.

Weak version

Patient: I'm not sure I'll manage. I live on my own, you see.

Nurse: I understand your concern. Don't worry, many patients feel the same. Um, you need to keep the dressing dry and do your exercises three times a day, and also you should take the painkillers before the exercises and call us if the wound is red or hot or there is discharge, OK?

Patient: Oh... right.

Nurse: Um, and the follow-up appointment is in two weeks.

Stronger version

Patient: I'm not sure I'll manage. I live on my own, you see.

Nurse: Going home alone so soon after an operation is a lot to think about. What worries you most about being at home?

Patient: Getting up and down, mainly. And what if the wound goes bad?

Nurse: Those are both important, so let's take them one at a time. First, the wound. There are two things to do: keep the dressing dry, and look at it each day. Does that sound manageable?

Patient: Yes, I can do that.

Nurse: Good. If it looks red, feels hot or starts to leak, please ring the ward. Now, about moving around safely at home...

What goes wrong in the weak version

A generic empathy line, followed by "don't worry", which dismisses the feeling. The cue "I live on my own" is never explored, so the explanation is not related to her situation. Several instructions arrive in one breath with no organisation and no pause. Her worried "Oh... right" is missed, and the topic change starts with "um".

What the stronger version adds

Empathy that names her actual situation; an open question that elicits her real concerns; an explanation related to those concerns; a signpost ("let's take them one at a time. First, the wound"); an organising technique ("two things"); a check of understanding; and a second signpost ("Now, about moving around"). It is not longer, just better structured.

Empathy phrases that stay genuine

Use them as frames and fill them with the patient's own words.

  • "It sounds as though [their worry] is what's bothering you most."
  • "That must have been frightening, especially when [detail they gave]."
  • "It's completely reasonable to feel [feeling] about [situation]."
  • "You mentioned [cue]. Can you tell me a bit more about that?"
  • "Let's see what we can do about that together."

Signposting phrases

Short is fine. The aim is that the patient always knows where the conversation is going.

  • "First, I'd like to ask about... Then we can talk about..."
  • "Now, if it's all right, I'd like to move on to..."
  • "Before we go on, is there anything else about that?"
  • "There are three things I'd like to explain. The first is..."
  • "So, to sum up what we've agreed..."

Common mistakes with empathy and signposting

Each of these makes a role-play sound rehearsed, even when the language is accurate.

One empathy line for every situation

"I understand your concern" said four times is a script. Vary it by reflecting the patient's own words.

"Don't worry" straight after empathy

It cancels the acknowledgement. Reassure with facts or a plan instead.

Empathy too early or too often

Opening with "I'm so sorry" before the patient has said anything is not a response to a cue. Wait for the feeling, then respond to it.

Changing topic without warning

Jumping from symptoms to medication confuses the patient. Signpost first, then change.

Information dumping after a cue

When a patient sounds worried mid-explanation, stop and acknowledge it. OET's guide says it is better not to finish every task than to rush information the patient cannot follow.

Key takeaway

Realistic empathy is specific and short, and it leads somewhere: reflect the patient's words, acknowledge the feeling, then signpost the next step. Plan where you will need it during the three minutes of preparation, and let the patient's cues decide when you use it.

OET Speaking empathy and signposting FAQ

Short answers to the questions candidates ask most about these two skills.

Is saying I understand your concern enough to show empathy in OET Speaking?

Not on its own. Empathy works when it reflects what the patient actually said and leads to help or a question. Repeating one fixed phrase sounds rehearsed, and OET guidance notes overuse of it in a sample role-play.

Which OET criteria cover empathy and signposting?

Empathy sits under Relationship-building, and picking up cues sits under Understanding and incorporating the patient perspective. Signposting changes in topic is part of Providing structure. All are clinical communication criteria scored from 0 to 3.

Can I write notes on the role-play card?

Yes. OET says you can make notes on the role-play card during the three minutes of preparation and refer to it during the role-play. Use that time to mark topic changes and likely emotional moments.

Do I lose marks if I do not finish every task on the card?

OET says there is no penalty for not completing every element, although covering more gives more evidence of your ability. A calm, well-signposted conversation is better than rushing to the end.
