OET on Computer vs Paper: Preparation Differences

OET says its paper and computer tests share the same structure, content, length and marking. What changes is how you read, answer, write and speak on the day, and that should change the last two weeks of your preparation.

By Dr Ahmed Hesham, medical doctor and OET educator · Published 27 October 2026

Three delivery modes

Same test, different habits to practise.

The real question: is one mode easier, and should I prepare differently?

Candidates often ask whether the computer test is easier. OET's answer is that paper and computer follow the same test structure, content, length and marking. It adds that candidates who prefer to type may find the computer more comfortable than handwriting. So the mode does not change what is assessed, but it does change the physical skills you need on the day.

Start with your regulator, not your preference

OET advises confirming with your recognising organisation which delivery modes it accepts before you book. OET@Home in particular may not be accepted everywhere. Our OET test dates and fees guide lists which major regulators accept OET@Home, plus booking deadlines and fees for each mode.

What actually differs between the three modes

These are the differences OET describes on its own pages. Each one has a preparation consequence.

OET on Paper

  • Taken at a secure, approved test centre using printed materials.
  • All four sub-tests on the same day at the same venue.
  • Listening with noise-cancelling headphones provided; Reading and Writing on paper.
  • Speaking face to face with an interlocutor in a private room.
  • You may bring non-mechanical pens, pencils, erasers and a sharpener; highlighters and scrap paper are not allowed.

OET on Computer

  • Listening, Reading and Writing on a computer at a test venue, on the same day.
  • Reading completed on screen; Writing typed on a standard keyboard.
  • Speaking is usually done from home through OET's Speaking Platform with webcam and microphone, on a different day up to five days before or after; in some countries it is at the venue.
  • An on-screen tutorial runs before the test starts.

OET@Home

  • The whole test online on your own computer with full remote proctoring.
  • Listening through a wired headset; Bluetooth headphones are not allowed.
  • Writing typed on your own device.
  • Speaking through the OET Speaking Platform with a live interlocutor.
  • Offered in countries where venue testing is not yet available.

Writing: handwriting vs typing

The task is the same in every mode: five minutes of reading time, then 40 minutes to write a letter of about 180 to 200 words, with the case notes available throughout. On paper, legibility and neat corrections matter, and OET allows all capitals as long as you are consistent. On screen, typing speed and accurate keyboard work matter. Spelling is assessed under Language in either mode, so a typing slip counts just as a handwriting slip does.

Reading and Listening on screen

Reading has 60 minutes: 15 for Part A and 45 for Parts B and C. On computer, OET explains that Listening Parts B and C show one question per page and you click to move on; in Part C you navigate with tabs on the left. OET's tips say that on computer you can cross off answer options by right-clicking and select answers with a left click, while on paper Parts B and C are answered by shading.

Speaking: room vs screen

Both formats have two role-plays, and before each one you get three minutes to read the card and make notes. On paper, you are face to face and can write on the role-play card. Online, the interlocutor shares the card on screen, does an ID and 360-degree room check, and headphones may not be worn. At a computer venue, OET mentions a noteboard for notes.

Booking and results

OET states that computer results arrive in as little as 48 hours after all four sub-tests are completed, with 95% released within six calendar days. Paper results are released from 13 days after the test date, or from five days in India and the Philippines. OET calls all of these timeframes indicative. Computer and OET@Home bookings stay open until seven days before test day.

How to choose your mode, step by step

Work through these in order. The first two can make the decision for you.

1. Check what your regulator accepts

Confirm the accepted modes, the minimum scores and whether results must come from one sitting. If OET@Home is not accepted, it is off the list.

2. Check availability for your profession and country

OET notes that not every date and mode is available in every country. Check your profession, venue and date in the official date finder before planning around one mode.

3. Time a typed letter and a handwritten letter

Write one practice letter each way under 40 minutes. Compare how much time you had left to check, and how many errors you found. That tells you more than a general preference does.

4. Weigh your deadline

If your registration deadline is close, faster indicative results and later booking deadlines can matter. If not, choose the format you perform best in.

Practice example: a two-week adjustment plan for each format

Practice example (an invented candidate, for teaching only). Leila is a nurse whose skills are already close to her target. She has two weeks before her test and wants to spend them getting used to the conditions of her chosen mode. Here is how her plan would differ.

If Leila sits OET on Paper

Week 1

  • Handwrite three letters under timed conditions: five minutes of reading time, then 40 minutes of writing.
  • Ask someone to read one letter and mark any word they cannot read at first glance.
  • Do printed Reading Part A tasks with a pencil only, no highlighter, in 15 minutes.
  • Practise shading answers neatly for Listening and Reading Parts B and C.

Week 2

  • One full mock of Listening, Reading and Writing back to back, then Speaking the same day, as on test day.
  • Two face-to-face role-plays with a partner; write brief notes on the card during the three minutes.
  • Final check routine: crossing out neatly instead of rewriting whole sentences.

If Leila sits OET on Computer

Week 1

  • Type three letters under timed conditions in a plain text editor with spell-check switched off, so errors are her own to catch.
  • Use OET's interactive computer sample tests to learn the layout, one-question-per-page navigation and tabs.
  • Read Reading Part B and C texts on a screen, not printed, to build on-screen stamina.
  • Practise right-click to cross off options and left-click to select.

Week 2

  • One full mock of Listening, Reading and Writing on screen without a break.
  • Two online role-plays by video call with the card shared on screen, no headphones, in a quiet, clear room.
  • Test her webcam, microphone and internet connection, and read OET's online Speaking user guide.
Why the plans differ

Neither plan teaches new skills. Both remove surprises: handwriting speed and shading on paper; screen reading, navigation and typing accuracy on computer; and for Speaking, either a face-to-face room or a webcam and a shared screen. If Leila chose OET@Home, she would follow the computer plan and also rehearse in the exact room and with the wired headset she will use.

Common mistakes when preparing for a specific mode

These are the problems we see most often when candidates do not adjust their final practice.

Practising only on paper for a computer test

Screen reading and typing feel different under time pressure. Use the interactive samples at least twice.

Relying on spell-check in practice

Spelling is assessed under Language. Practise with corrections switched off so you learn to check your own letter.

Planning on highlighters or scrap paper

OET's test regulations list both as items you cannot bring into the test room. Practise with the materials you will really have.

Leaving the online Speaking set-up to the last day

Room checks, ID checks and equipment problems add stress. Read the online Speaking guide and test your device in advance.

Booking a mode your regulator does not accept

Check accepted modes before paying, especially for OET@Home.

Key takeaway

The content and marking are the same, so choose the mode your regulator accepts and you perform best in. Then spend the last two weeks practising in that mode's conditions: handwriting and shading for paper; typing, on-screen reading and online Speaking for computer and OET@Home.

OET computer vs paper FAQ

Short answers to the questions candidates ask before choosing a mode.

Is OET on computer easier than OET on paper?

OET says both follow the same test structure, content, length and marking. Candidates who prefer typing may find the computer more comfortable, but the level required for each grade is the same.

How is the Speaking sub-test delivered in each mode?

On paper, Speaking is face to face with an interlocutor at the venue. For OET on Computer it is usually done from home through the OET Speaking Platform, or at the venue in some countries. OET@Home Speaking is online with a live interlocutor.

Can I take notes in OET Speaking on computer?

Yes. OET says you have three minutes before each role-play to read the card, make notes and ask about unfamiliar words. At a computer venue a noteboard is provided for this.

Which mode gives results faster?

OET states computer and OET@Home results can arrive in as little as 48 hours after all four sub-tests, while paper results come from 13 days after the test in most regions. These timeframes are indicative.
